# Instructional Design

## Ask whether training is the right intervention

Much requested training addresses a problem it cannot fix: unclear process, missing tool, wrong incentive, or a task done twice a year that should be a checklist instead.

Training changes capability. If the person already knows and still cannot do it, the obstacle is elsewhere and a course will waste everyone's time while appearing to respond.

## Start from what they must be able to do

Write the objective as an observable action: "can reconcile a bank statement and explain each difference", not "understands reconciliation". If you cannot say how you would see it, you cannot design toward it or assess it.

Everything in the material either serves an objective or comes out.

## Practice with feedback is the mechanism; everything else is preparation

People learn by attempting and finding out how they did. Content sets that up. A session that is all explanation and no attempt feels efficient and transfers little.

Aim for most of the time spent doing, with feedback close to the attempt. Delayed feedback teaches much less.

## Design for forgetting

Learning decays fast without retrieval. Spacing, a small amount of revisiting, and a job aid for the parts nobody should memorise beat a longer single session at any total time budget.

Not everything should be learned. Some things should be looked up, and deciding which is design work.

## Watch out for

- Assessment testing recall of the material rather than the objective.
- A course covering everything known rather than what is needed.
- Learner effort spent on navigation and formatting instead of the subject.
- Accessibility retrofitted after production.
- Success measured by completion and satisfaction, which correlate weakly with capability.

## Finishing

Training was confirmed as the right intervention. Objectives are observable actions. Most time is practice with close feedback. Spacing and job aids handle retention. Assessment tests the objective.
