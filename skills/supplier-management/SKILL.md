# Supplier Management

## Unit price is the smallest part of total cost

Freight, duty, payment terms, minimum order quantity, defect rate, lead-time variability, the cost of your own time managing them. A cheaper unit price with a 4% defect rate and erratic lead times is more expensive, often by a lot.

Compare landed cost per usable unit. That is the only comparison that means anything.

## Single-sourcing is a decision that must be made explicitly

Sometimes it is right: better price, better relationship, genuine uniqueness. But it is a bet, and it should be a named one with a qualified alternative identified even if unused.

The time to find a second supplier is not while the first has stopped delivering.

## Measure performance or do not claim to manage it

On-time-in-full, defect rate, responsiveness, quoted versus actual lead time. Reviewed on a schedule, shared with the supplier.

A supplier who has never seen their own numbers cannot improve, and a relationship without measurement runs on how recent the last complaint was.

## Escalate on a path, not on frustration

Operational contact, then their manager, then commercial, then the contract. Each with a stated timeframe. Jumping to the contract on a first miss burns the relationship you will still need next month; never escalating means nothing changes.

## Watch out for

- Payment terms conceded for a small discount, and the working-capital effect not calculated.
- Quality agreed verbally and not specified, so "defect" is arguable.
- A supplier who is a reseller you cannot see behind, with hidden concentration risk.
- Concentration by geography or logistics route rather than by supplier name.
- Long relationships never re-tendered, where the price drifted well off market.

## Finishing

Comparisons use landed cost per usable unit. Single-sourcing is deliberate with an identified alternative. Performance is measured, scheduled and shared. Escalation follows a path with timeframes.
