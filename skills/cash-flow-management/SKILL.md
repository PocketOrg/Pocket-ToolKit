# Cash Flow Management

## Profitable businesses run out of money

Profit is an accounting result over a period; cash is what is in the bank on a Tuesday. A growing business consumes cash — stock and wages go out before customers pay — so the faster it grows, the worse the gap.

Anyone reading only the profit line will be surprised, and the surprise arrives as a missed payroll.

## Forecast weekly, thirteen weeks out, and roll it

Monthly is too coarse to see a mid-month trough that breaks you. Thirteen weeks is far enough to act — chase, delay, borrow — and near enough to be worth trusting.

Roll it every week against actuals. The variance teaches you where your assumptions are wrong, which is the real output.

## The cash conversion cycle is the lever

How long stock sits, how long customers take to pay, how long you take to pay. Improving any of the three releases cash permanently without a sale.

Debtor days is usually the quickest win and is mostly an administrative problem: invoice on the day, state terms, chase on a schedule rather than when it becomes urgent.

## Know the floor and what happens near it

Name the minimum balance below which you cannot operate, and what you will do at that level: which payments delay, what facility draws, what gets cut. Decided in advance and written down.

Deciding under pressure produces the expensive choices — the invoice-discounting deal signed in a week.

## Watch out for

- Tax and payroll treated as flexible timing. They are not, and the penalties compound.
- A single large customer whose late payment is an existential event.
- Growth funded by supplier credit that has no headroom left.
- Seasonality forgotten because last year's trough is out of memory.
- A forecast built once and never compared to what happened.

## Finishing

A rolling thirteen-week weekly forecast exists and is compared to actuals. The conversion cycle is measured with a named target. The minimum balance and the actions at it are written down.
