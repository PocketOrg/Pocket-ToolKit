# Employee Onboarding

## Day one is a signal about the whole organisation

An account that does not work, no laptop, nobody expecting them: the new person concludes something accurate about how things are run, and it is hard to undo.

Everything mechanical — access, equipment, payroll, a named first contact — is done before they arrive, not on the morning.

## Give a real task in the first week

Not training, not shadowing: something small that ships. It converts abstract onboarding into orientation with a purpose, and it surfaces the gaps in your setup faster than any checklist.

The first task should be genuinely useful and genuinely small. A trivial task insults; a large one strands.

## Front-load context, not process

What the business actually does, who the customer is, what is currently hard, who decides what. Process documentation can be read when needed; context cannot be looked up and is what makes their judgement usable.

## Thirty, sixty, ninety with real questions

Fixed check-ins with specific questions — what is still unclear, what has taken longer than it should, what surprised you — surface fixable problems while they are still cheap.

The new person is the only one who can see your onboarding clearly, and only for about a month. Write down what they say; it is the input for the next hire.

## Watch out for

- A buddy assigned who was not asked and has no time.
- Access granted broadly on day one because narrowing it later is effort.
- Onboarding that ends at the equipment handover.
- The same question asked by every new joiner and never written down.
- Probation reviews held with no record of what was agreed at the start.

## Finishing

Everything mechanical was ready before arrival. A small real task shipped in week one. Context came before process. Check-ins happened and their answers changed the next onboarding.
