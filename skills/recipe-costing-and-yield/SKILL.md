# Recipe Costing and Yield

## Yield is where the money is, not the ingredient list

A recipe costed from raw purchase weight is wrong by whatever gets trimmed, evaporated, dropped or left in the bowl. Butter at 100% and flour at 100% is a plausible-looking cost that no real kitchen achieves.

Cost the *yielded* quantity: what reaches the customer, divided into what you bought. For anything trimmed or reduced, measure it once in your own kitchen rather than using a book figure.

## Waste is a line item, and it is not the same as yield loss

Yield loss is inherent to the process. Waste is unsold product, failed batches and expiry. Both belong in the cost, and separating them tells you which one to attack — a low-yield method is a recipe problem, high waste is a forecasting problem.

Bakery is unforgiving here: the whole product line has a one-day shelf life, so a 20% end-of-day discard is a fifth of the batch cost with no revenue.

## Price on contribution, not on a fixed multiple

A blanket "three times food cost" prices some items off the market and gives others away. What matters is the cash contribution per item and per hour of oven or bench time.

A low-margin loaf that fills the oven while the high-margin item proves may be the right thing to keep. Decide with the arithmetic in front of you.

## Scaling breaks at bakers' percentages, not at multiplication

Doubling a dough does not double mixing time, proving time or oven load, and hydration behaves differently at volume. Scale by bakers' percentage against total flour weight, then re-test — the first scaled batch is a trial, not production.

Salt, yeast and leavening are where naive scaling fails most visibly.

## Watch out for

- Ingredient prices that moved since the recipe was costed, especially dairy, eggs and flour.
- Packaging, labels and bags left out of the cost entirely.
- Labour costed as an overhead when one item takes four times the bench time of another.
- The same sub-recipe costed differently in two places.
- Menu prices ending in a psychologically chosen number that has drifted below cost.

## Finishing

Every cost uses yielded quantities measured in this kitchen. Waste and yield loss are separated. Prices state contribution per item, not just a multiple.
