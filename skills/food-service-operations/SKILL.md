# Food Service Operations

## The constraint moves, and you must know where it is

At any moment one thing limits throughput: a station, an oven, the pass, the till, the dish pit. Adding staff anywhere else changes nothing and makes the space worse.

Watch where the queue forms. That is the constraint, and it moves between dayparts — the morning constraint is rarely the evening one.

## Prep is a forecast, and forecasts are checkable

Prep too little and you sell out of the thing people came for. Prep too much and you discard margin. Both are failures of the same estimate.

Keep the actual against the forecast by day of week. Weather, local events and paydays are real signals; a manager's feel usually is not, and the record settles it either way.

## Consistency comes from the build, not from the recipe

Two cooks reading the same recipe produce different plates. What removes the variance is a specified build: same scoop, same order, same finish, checked at the pass.

Write the spec down with a photograph. A verbal standard drifts by a shift and is unrecoverable by the next one.

## Recovering a bad experience beats preventing complaints

Things go wrong in every service. What decides whether the guest returns is what happened next — acknowledged fast, fixed without argument, by someone empowered to fix it.

Give the shift a standing allowance to resolve a problem on the spot. Escalation for a wrong order costs more in goodwill than the dish.

## Watch out for

- Holding times exceeded because nothing is timestamped.
- The cleaning schedule slipping first when a shift is short — the most expensive corner to cut.
- Allergen handling treated as a labelling task rather than a preparation one.
- Temperature logs completed retrospectively, which makes them worthless as evidence.
- One person on the rota whose absence stops service.

## Finishing

The current constraint is identified and staffed. Prep tracks forecast against actual. Every repeated item has a written, photographed build.
