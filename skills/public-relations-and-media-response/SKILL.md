# Public Relations and Media Response

## A pitch is a story for their readers, not news about you

A journalist's question is always the same: why would my audience care today. A funding round, a hire or a milestone answers that only if it evidences something the audience already argues about.

Lead with the claim and the evidence. Include the data, the name, the number. A pitch that requires a follow-up call to become interesting will not get one.

## Speed and accuracy trade against each other, and silence is a position

In a bad story, the first hours set the frame. But a wrong first statement becomes the story itself and forces a correction cycle you will not control.

So publish what you can stand behind: what happened, what you are doing, when you will say more. Say what you do not yet know rather than filling it. "No comment" reads as confirmation to every audience.

## Never speculate on cause while it is being established

The strongest position in a difficult story is a narrow, true statement with a named next update. The weakest is an early theory that later turns out wrong, because it converts one story into two.

## Correct fast and completely

A partial correction extends the story. Correct the record plainly, once, in the same channel, without relitigating. Nobody has ever been damaged by a clean correction as much as by a defended error.

## Watch out for

- Embargoes broken by an internal share, which ends a relationship with that journalist permanently.
- Spokespeople who have not been briefed on the difficult question.
- A holding statement that promises a timeline you cannot meet.
- Internal communication that will obviously leak, drafted as if it will not.
- Legal and communications giving contradictory advice with no one reconciling them.

## Finishing

Every pitch names why this audience cares now, with evidence. Statements contain only what can be stood behind, plus a next-update time. Corrections were made plainly and once.
