# Editorial Standards

## Separate the four passes, or do none of them well

Substance: is it true and does it hold together. Structure: is it in the right order. Line: does each sentence work. Proof: spelling, punctuation, consistency.

Mixed together, you rewrite sentences that get cut and miss a factual error while fixing a comma. Substance first, always — it is the pass most likely to change everything downstream.

## Every factual claim gets a source or a hedge

A number, a date, a superlative or an attributed quote is either verified or softened. "The largest in Europe" needs a source and a date; without one it becomes "among the largest", or it goes.

This is the difference between editing and proofreading, and it is where an editor earns their place.

## A style guide exists to stop re-deciding

Serial comma, capitalisation of job titles, number formatting, date format, how the company name is written. Decide once, write it down, apply it. The point is not correctness — several answers are defensible — it is that nobody spends attention on it twice.

## Edit toward the writer's voice, not your own

The test for a line edit is whether the sentence says the thing more clearly, not whether it sounds more like you. Where a writer's construction is unusual but works, leave it.

Change of meaning always goes back to the writer as a question, never as a silent fix.

## Watch out for

- Hedging inserted so heavily the piece stops claiming anything.
- Passive constructions hiding who did something, in the exact places it matters.
- Headings and body text drifting apart after a restructure.
- Links unchecked, or pointing somewhere that no longer says what was cited.
- Consistency enforced so hard that a quotation gets silently altered.

## Finishing

Substance was checked before line-level work. Every factual claim is sourced or hedged. Style decisions came from the guide. Meaning changes went back to the writer.
